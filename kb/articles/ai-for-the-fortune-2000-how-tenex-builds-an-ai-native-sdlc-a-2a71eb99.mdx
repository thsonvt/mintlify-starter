## Full Article

Last year, Alex Lieberman and his cofounder joined us to explain Tenexs unusual bet: pay AI engineers for the work they deliver, rather than the hours they spend at a keyboard. If AI makes an engineer faster, an hourly rate can make the engineer less money for the same job. Tenex wanted the incentives to run the other way.At AIE New York, Alex came back with CJ Hess to talk about what happens when you scale that idea into an engineering organization. We also put one of our own experiments on the table: Kill My SaaS, where we asked builders to replace a conference speaker-management tool we were quoted $43,000 a year to use.Both stories raise the same question: when building gets cheaper, how do you decide what good work is worth?Paying for output when the job keeps changingTenex still uses story points as an important part of engineering compensation. Alex says the feared failure modespeople inflating estimates or shipping bad code to rack up pointshavent been the biggest problem. Tenexs AI strategists are responsible for customer retention and growth, giving them an incentive to push back on work that doesnt serve the client.The harder question is how to reward work that doesnt fit neatly into a ticket: understanding a customers problem, designing an architecture, or deciding what should be built in the first place. CJ describes spending less time directly coding as models get better, and more time getting the customers context into a form the model can use. A compensation system designed around shipped tasks has to make room for that work too.Alex says Tenex has grown to 75 people, with customers split between Fortune 2000 companies and mid-market businesses. The firm started as an AI-powered development shop; increasingly, customers are asking for help with AI transformation across the company. Jump to the compensation discussion MetaHarness: keeping the project in the repoCJ walks through Tenexs MetaHarness: a shared set of skills, project documents and a CLI around the work. Plans and decisions are checked into Git alongside the code. When an agent comes back to a task, it can read not just what was built, but why the team chose to build it that way.Tenexs own AI-native SDLC article shows the idea as a project record that humans and agents work against, with views into Linear, GitHub and client updates:Source: Tenex, Building an AI-Native SDLC.The useful distinction is between making agents write code faster and making the surrounding work legible: the plan, acceptance criteria, decisions and evidence. Tenexs article also notes the cost: that much structure can be overkill for a small team on one project. CJ explains the workflow Kill My SaaS: the cost of owning the thing you buildOur Kill My SaaS episode covered 72 submissions to replace our speaker-management software. The team that won with our human judges came 19th on our AI judges leaderboard. It was a useful reminder that checking whether software satisfies a list of scenarios and judging whether people want to use it are different jobs.I brought the project to Alex as a real example of a company choosing to build its own software. His pushback: the further upmarket you go, the more expensive and risky it becomes to rip out an established system. And even for a smaller tool, someone has to maintain it. Is the saving big enough to justify that ongoing attention?My answer is that the upside isnt just the bill. Our team has lived with a mediocre product for a long time. If we own the workflow, we can make it better for speakers and operators and iterate in minutes instead of waiting months for a vendor. That potential has to be weighed against the maintenance Alex describes. And as frontier models improve, how much of the underlying task gets absorbed by general-purpose tools?I pushed back with examples like Harvey and OpenEvidence. Domain-specific data, specialized interfaces, integrations and user habits can matter even when the underlying model is available to everyone. Bloomberg is a useful comparison: a trader can get market data elsewhere, but the terminal brings data, workflows, communication and existing habits together.CJ draws a distinction between a vertical product whose main feature is an agent performing a task, and a tool that improves the actual workflow around that task. That leaves a more interesting question than whether vertical AI wins or loses: which products become a durable place people do their work, and which become a feature of the agent they already use?
